GT2 port · physics · step rate

30 Hz and 120 Hz

GT2 runs its race 30 times a second: every force, every input and every timer moves once per tick of 2184/65536 s. The port's classic physics keeps exactly that. Modern physics uses new formulas in real units, and cuts every GT2 tick into four world ticks of 546/65536 s. This page separates the two changes. A third setup, modern forced back to 30 Hz, shows what the step rate does on its own and what the formulas do. Then the math shows where the rate matters: the stiff loops (ABS, wheel spin), the heading step, the inputs, and the per-step jolt.

The short version. On a straight, modern at 30 Hz and modern at 120 Hz are within about 0.7 % of each other. The 0.5–1 % gap to classic comes from the new formulas, not the step. The rate matters in the stiff, fast loops: the ABS releasing a locked wheel, wheel spin at launch, and the 30 Hz staircase in every input. There, 1/30 s is too long a step. Three setups are measured: classic 30 Hz, modern at 30 Hz, modern at 120 Hz.

The same second, cut in four

The time step

GT2 tick     dt = 2184 / 65536 s  = 0.033325 s   (30 a "second")
world tick   dt =  546 / 65536 s  = 0.008331 s   (546 × 4 = 2184 exactly)
race clock   +100 units a tick  →  +25 a world tick   (3000 units/s both)

2184 is round(65536/30). 1/30 s would be 2184.53, so a GT2 "second" of ticks is 0.99976 s. Modern keeps GT2's tick exactly: four world ticks add up to the same 2184, so lap times and the race clock are unchanged by the rate.

Why a shorter step helps

explicit step     x(t+dt) = x + v·dt,   v(t+dt) = v + a(x,v)·dt
error per second  ∝ dt          → 4× smaller at 120 Hz
a stiff loop      v' = −λ·v   is stable only if  λ·dt < 2
                  oscillates (overshoots zero) if  λ·dt > 1

Most of a car is slow next to 1/30 s: the body and the suspension don't care much. A few loops are fast. A tire's grip acts on a wheel's spin within milliseconds, and the ABS reacts to that slip. For those, λ·dt at 1/30 s is past 1, so the step overshoots, and that overshoot is what you feel. At 120 Hz, λ·dt is four times smaller.

Acceleration: the formulas, not the rate

Eight stock cars on an endless flat straight with full throttle and the wheel held straight. Each row shows modern's change against classic, at 30 Hz and at 120 Hz. The zero line is classic. Times are read once per 30 Hz frame, so they're good to about ±0.033 s, which is 0.2–0.8 % of a 0-100 run.

Median against classic: 0-100 is −0.5 % at 30 Hz and −1.0 % at 120 Hz. Top speed is +0.7 % at both. 400 m is −0.35 % and −0.5 %. Top speed doesn't depend on the rate at all, since it's a balance of drive force and drag. The 0-100 difference between the rates is about one frame of timing.

Launch: the force at the end of the step

A spinning wheel and the tire's push on the body have to agree. Modern steps the wheel implicitly, solving for the end-of-step spin ω′. At first, the body still took the force at the start-of-step spin ω. The gap is one step of the wheel's change:

body force before   F(ω)
wheel balanced at   F(ω′),   ω′ = ω + α·dt
lag per step        ΔF ≈ S·α·dt     (S = dF/dω, the tire's slope)

During a launch the slope S is steep and α is large, so the lag cost 5–12 % of the launch force, even at 120 Hz. The fix is an integration correction: the body now takes the end-of-step force. No constant moved.

Turning: GT2's heading step

GT2 turns the car's heading once a tick. It multiplies the yaw rate by dt and keeps only whole heading units (4096 to a turn), truncated toward zero:

dh = (yaw × dt) >> 23            (heading units per tick)
one unit per tick  =  (2π / 4096) / (2184 / 65536 s)
                   =  0.046 rad/s  =  2.64 °/s
GT2's turning rate =  0.046 × trunc(r / 0.046)

So the heading turns at the true yaw rate rounded down to whole steps of 0.046 rad/s. Below one step it doesn't turn at all. Without this rule, modern turned 8 % more than classic on the same inputs (path gain 1.133 on the flat plane, 1.079 on the license demos). Modern now keeps the step as a rate, so it's the same at 30 Hz or 120 Hz: path gain 0.999.

Applied as "one unit per step" at 120 Hz, the step would become 4 times coarser (0.184 rad/s). So it's kept in seconds, not ticks.

Braking: the ABS loop

GT2's ABS looks at each wheel's slip once a tick and sets the brake for the next. How much one tick of braking moves the slip is the loop gain:

L = g · grip · r² · dt / (I_w · v)

g     ABS gain           r    tire radius
grip  tire grip (N)      I_w  wheel inertia (kg·m²)
v     speed (m/s)        dt   the step

The brake torque cancels out. With L above about 3 the loop can't settle. The wheel locks within a tick, the ABS lets go completely, the free wheel spins back up past the road speed and pushes the car forward, and the cycle repeats at 10–15 Hz. L grows with dt, so at 120 Hz every car's L is a quarter of its 30 Hz value. A car at L = 8 on classic sits at 2 on modern.

The cars with the highest L are heavy front-engine, rear-drive cars. Their front wheels are undriven, carry the most load and have little inertia. A full tune makes it worse twice over: more grip, and lighter drive-line parts that cut wheel inertia by about 37 %.

At 30 Hz even modern's formulas show GT2's reversal: the tune stops longer. At 60 Hz it's gone. Fleet-wide, FR cars with a front L of 3.5 or more stop 18 % shorter on modern. Modern at 120 Hz holds the slip at the brake peak on every car measured (0.97–0.98 of grip).

The eight cars here are mostly low-L: modern stops 0.3–0.5 % shorter at the median, and the rate moves them under ±1 %.
